Expected loss of choosing B is the average conversion rate you give up if B turns out to be the worse arm, weighted by how likely that is. It treats small mistakes as cheap and big mistakes as expensive, which a bare probability does not.

Most dashboards show this number and teams peek at it daily. Peeking breaks it: checking repeatedly and stopping when it "looks significant" inflates the real false-positive rate far above 5%. The Bayesian quantities above stay valid whenever you look, which is the point of this calculator.

5. Sample-size planner

How many visitors per arm do you need so that the test usually ends with a clear Bayesian call? This runs a Monte Carlo: it assumes true rates, simulates trials, and counts how often P(B beats A) clears your bar. That is simulated assurance, not classical power: the decision rule is Bayesian and the assumed truth is a point, so read it as planning guidance, not a guarantee.

Simulated data uses a normal approximation to the binomial (120 simulated trials per grid point); fine for planning, not for final analysis.

How it works

  • Posterior. Beta(a, b) prior with s successes in n trials gives Beta(a + s, b + n - s). Beta is the conjugate prior for the binomial, so the update is exact arithmetic.
  • P(B beats A). Numerical integration of the B density times the A CDF over [0, 1]. Works for any prior, unlike the closed form which needs integer parameters.
  • Expected loss. Choosing B costs E[max(pA - pB, 0)] under the joint posterior. The inner integral has an exact form in beta CDFs, so one numerical quadrature finishes it.
  • Decision. Ship an arm when its expected loss is under your threshold of caring. Method follows the VWO SmartStats framework (Stucchio, 2015); the Python analysis behind this page cross-checks the quadrature against the exact closed form and Monte Carlo.
