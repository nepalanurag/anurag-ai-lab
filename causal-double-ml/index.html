Does 401(k) Eligibility Raise Wealth?

A four-estimator causal comparison: naive OLS, OLS with controls, propensity-score matching, and double machine learning.

Overview Methods Results Limitations

Overview

Being offered a 401(k) plan is not random: higher-income, older, more educated workers are more likely to be eligible, and those same traits predict wealth with or without a retirement plan. This project estimates the causal effect of eligibility on net financial assets with four methods and scores each one against a known true effect of $8,000, because the data are simulated from a documented process (the classic Wooldridge 401k file could not be fetched from any public mirror; the attempts are listed in data/build_data.py).

Eligible workers earn on average $79,209 vs $46,439 for the non-eligible. That gap is selection, not causation, and the naive estimate below treats it as causation.

Methods

All four methods target the average causal effect of eligibility on net financial assets, under unconfoundedness (no unmeasured common causes) and overlap (every worker type has some chance of being eligible or not). Both hold by construction in the simulation.

MethodIdeaWhen it is valid
Naive OLSRegress wealth on eligibility onlyOnly under random assignment (not the case here)
OLS with controlsAdd income, age, education, etc. linearlyOnly if confounders act linearly (income is concave here)
Propensity-score matchingLogistic score, 1:1 nearest-neighbor with replacement and caliper; average within-pair wealth gapsIf matches are truly alike on confounders; balance is checked
Double machine learningCross-fitted random forests learn E[wealth|X] and E[eligibility|X] out-of-fold; regress wealth residuals on eligibility residuals; robust SEIf the ML models capture the confounding; no functional-form guess needed

The DML is implemented by hand with scikit-learn (no econml): K-fold split, out-of-fold nuisance predictions, residual-on-residual slope with a heteroskedasticity-robust standard error.

Monte Carlo check: bias and coverage over 30 fresh datasets

One dataset is one draw, so the full analysis was repeated on 30 fresh simulated datasets (n = 4,000 each). A well-calibrated 95% interval should cover the truth about 95% of the time.

Reading the Monte Carlo table. The naive interval never covers the truth, and the linear-controls interval covers only 30% of the time. Matching is nearly unbiased on average but its intervals cover just 43%: the paired standard error treats reused controls as independent, so it understates the real variability. DML is the only method whose intervals cover at roughly the nominal rate (90%).

Limitations

  • Unconfoundedness is assumed, not shown. If an unmeasured variable (financial literacy, employer generosity) drives both eligibility and wealth, all four estimates are biased and no adjustment on these covariates can fix that.
  • Simulated data. The data-generating process is realistic but invented; the numbers describe the simulation, not the US labor market. The methods and the comparison are real.
  • Matching standard errors are optimistic: they treat the matches as fixed and the propensity score as known.
  • Constant treatment effect ($8,000 for everyone) keeps the four methods comparable; real effects vary.
  • Overlap holds by construction (propensities clipped to [0.02, 0.98]).
